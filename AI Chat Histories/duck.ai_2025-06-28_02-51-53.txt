1. The traditional “rule”  
   • In Latin, the verb “to be” (sum, es, est . . . ) is a true copula, and grammarians argue that whatever comes after it should be in the nominative case (the “subject” case).  
   • By analogy, early English grammarians insisted that after the verb be you use the nominative (“I,” “he,” “she,” “we,” “they”) and never the objective (“me,” “him,” “her,” “us,” “them”).

   • English no longer has a robust case system.  We don’t routinely mark subject vs. object on nouns, and even on pronouns the distinction is weakening.

      – “That was him.”  
      – “It wasn’t her.”  
      – “It’s me.”  
   • You literally never hear “It is I” in ordinary conversation—unless someone’s parodying a stiff, old-fashioned register.

3. How grammarians and style guides approach it today  
   • Descriptive grammarians (who observe real usage) note that “It’s me” has been standard in English for centuries.  
   • Most modern style manuals say that in informal contexts “It’s me” is fine.
